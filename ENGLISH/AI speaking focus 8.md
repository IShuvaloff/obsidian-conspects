# 8. Grammar Discovery — Present Perfect vs Past Simple

You were given three sentences:

1. **I've lived here for 15 years.** ✅  
2. **I lived there for 15 years, but then I moved away.** ✅  
3. **I've lived there for 15 years, but then I moved away.** ❌

The key idea:

> **for** only expresses duration. It does not choose the tense.

The tense depends on whether the situation is still connected to now or completely finished.

---

# 9. Present Perfect vs Past Simple

| Tense | When to Use It | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Present Perfect** | situation started in the past and continues to now | **I've worked here for five years.** |
| **Past Simple** | situation is completely finished | **I worked there for five years, but then I left.** |

### Still true now

**I've lived here for fifteen years.**

### Finished

**I lived there for fifteen years, but then I moved away.**

---

# 10. Important Rule About “For”

| Structure | Correct? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present Perfect + **for** | ✅ | **I've worked here for five years.** |
| Past Simple + **for** | ✅ | **I worked there for five years.** |

So:

> **for does not automatically mean Present Perfect.**

It simply introduces duration.

---

# 11. Past Perfect

## Structure

**had + past participle**

Example:

**I had lived there for fifteen years before I moved.**

| Earlier Past | Later Past |
|---|---|
| **I had lived there for fifteen years** | **before I moved.** |

Past Perfect is useful when you want to make the sequence of two past events especially clear.

---

# 12. Past Simple vs Past Perfect

| Version | Meaning / Nuance |
|---|---|
| **I lived there for fifteen years before I moved.** | Clear and natural; Past Simple is enough. |
| **I had lived there for fifteen years before I moved.** | Emphasizes that the first period happened before the second past event. |

# 27. Active Vocabulary Review System

From now on, vocabulary should be maintained as a growing active bank.

Each lesson should add:

- useful new words;
- collocations;
- phrasal verbs;
- idioms;
- fixed expressions;
- natural sentence frames.

And every lesson should recycle older items.

| Format | Example |
|---|---|
| Definition → phrase | “Talk to an old friend after a long time.” → **catch up** |
| Phrase → meaning | “drained” → extremely tired |
| Situation → phrase | “Find a word in a dictionary.” → **look it up** |
| Fill in the blank | “I could have ___...” → **sworn** |
| Error detection | *We catched up.* → **We caught up.** |
| Create a sentence | Use **on my own** in a new sentence. |
| Contrast | **look up** vs **search** |
| Surprise recall | Old vocabulary appears unexpectedly in conversation. |

Recommended duration:

**5–10 minutes per lesson**

Recommended amount:

**around 10–15 items**, depending on difficulty.

# Final Reflection

Lesson 8 revealed an important shift in the course.

The challenge is no longer simply learning more and more new words. The next stage is **retrieval**:

> Can you recall the right phrase spontaneously, several days or weeks later, while speaking?

That is the difference between passive vocabulary and active vocabulary.

The new vocabulary-recycling routine should therefore become a permanent part of the course.

At the same time, today’s grammar work showed strong progress in reasoning about tense rather than memorizing isolated rules.

The key idea was:

> **“for” expresses duration, but the tense tells us whether the situation is still connected to now, finished in the past, or happened before another past event.**
